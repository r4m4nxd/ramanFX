## 1. Drawing levels and entry triggers

- **Two lines, not one.** Don't try to pick the exact turn. Wrap the level with two lines and bet on a turn *in that area*. A long buys at the upper (proximal) demand line, with the protective sell stop just below the lower line (p81). Size the position so the risk is one you can accept.
- **Any signal is fine, but only AT a level.** A momentum candle followed by a reversal candle at the level, or a CCI overbought / oversold reading at the level, both count as triggers. The signal matters less than where it fires (p81).
- **Hierarchy of levels (p88-89):**
  - A **pivot** (price could not stay there) shows a large imbalance, so it is the strongest kind of level. The first pullback into it ("C") is the low-risk entry.
  - A **cluster** (a sideways block of trading) means supply and demand were only mildly out of balance. Price often slices "like a hot knife through butter" on the first return ("D").
  - Rule: "The greater the supply and demand imbalance, the greater the opportunity."
- **A base in the middle of a move is not quality (p94).** A sideways range with a rally before it and a rally after it failed on a gap open. The pivot low below it (where price "explodes" away) is the quality level.
- **"Focus on price levels price can't go to or stay at" (p96).** The S&P 1263-1264 demand was special because of what did *not* happen there: it traded to 1262.50 once and left quickly. Revisit = entry, target 1270+.
- **Wicks = footprints of sellers (p112).** NASDAQ supply at 1175, which was also the **overnight (globex) high**, so the level had confluence.
- **Find the strong move first (p155-157). Explicitly flagged as new to readers.** Don't scan for bases ("A"), because they are everywhere. Instead:
  - Step 1: find a **strong momentum move** ("B").
  - Step 2: follow it back to its **origin**. That is the level.
  - Then enter on the revisit ("C") and target the opposing level ("D").
- **Sideways basing = a hidden imbalance (p115-116).** Three candles basing look like balance, but the equation was always out of balance and needed time to clear. The big move away is institutional, because retail lacks the buying power. "Where smart money buys is where retail sells."

## 2. Odds enhancers ("Trading and Gambling", p102-103)

When to buy: **a pullback to demand**, scored by:

1. **In an uptrend.**
2. **Profit margin of at least 3:1** (reward / risk).
3. **A quality demand level:**
   - a. a **strong rally away** (big imbalance);
   - b. **not retested yet** (greatest odds);
   - c. **near larger-timeframe demand**;
   - d. a **pivot low, well placed on the larger supply / demand curve**.
4. **How price arrives:** a **stair-step decline** into demand = low odds. A **sharp drop** into demand = much higher odds.

More enhancers come from indicators and oscillators (not listed). Assess the odds first, *then* decide how much capital to risk. That is the casino model: fixed rules that are not changed after a loss.

The arrival point is restated on p112-113. The rally into supply was "built with NO DEMAND levels", just big green candles, and Seiden **requires** such strong arrivals because the empty space below becomes the profit margin.

## 3. Freshness and number of tests

- **XLT rule: do NOT buy at a support level that has been tested more than once (p107).** Buy on the **first or second** revisit only, and only with a desirable profit margin (p108). Ignore books that say to wait for 2-3 tests.
- Each revisit lets more of the resting orders fill, so each later test raises the odds of a break. Dow monthly 7500-8000 demand example: be cautious until price reaches **"fresh"** demand (p107-108).
- **Depth of penetration** into a level is mentioned as an XLT factor for judging the remaining orders, but it is not explained (p107).
- **Successive tests and the breakout (p83-84).** Supply at a horizontal level gives a big drop on the 1st touch, a shallower drop on the 2nd and 3rd, then **basing sideways at the level on the 4th**. Sellers are used up, so buy the breakout (tree-chopping analogy). Do the mirror image for shorts.

## 4. Breakout entries (p82-86)

- **S/R breakout:** the setup is a series of shrinking reactions at the level, then basing at the level, then entry on the break (above). This is the most popular entry at OTA.
- **Trend-line breakout:** a trend line needs **two points**. Enter on the break of the down-trend line (long) or the up-trend line (short). **Target = the origin of the whole trend** (where the decline or rally began).
- The logic is the same in every market ("people and price"). The rookie-card analogy: once the last card at a price sells, the price must rise.

## 5. Gaps

### Why gaps matter
- A gap is "the picture of the ultimate supply and demand imbalance." Demand above the prior close gives a gap up; supply above it gives a gap down (p138).
- Gaps are the most obvious way to spot a novice (p108).

### Gap × trend checklist (p104-106; restated as four rules on p110)

| Context | Gap | Action | Odds / fill speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Downtrend | Gap **up into supply** | Short | **Highest odds**; fills very fast |
| Downtrend | Gap down into demand | Long is possible | Lower odds; almost always fills, but slower; may even be a short with a big margin |
| Uptrend | Gap up into supply | Short only with low risk and a high target | Lower odds; fills within the day or soon after; can be a buy with a big margin |
| Uptrend | Gap **down into demand** | Long | **Highest odds**; fills very fast |

The key question is **who is making the biggest mistake**. The NASDAQ example (p109): a gap up *after a rally, in a downtrend, into supply* is "the most potent novice trader signal", and the next day was a big down day.

### Professional gap vs novice gap (p138-142)
- **Professional gap:** a gap *after a move, against that move*. It starts at the beginning of moves and ignites them (in an uptrend: a gap down after a rally, usually at resistance, which ends the uptrend).
  - Action: **join it on a pullback to the origin of the gap**, if the profit margin is significant. Price often fills the gap quickly and then continues.
- **Novice gap:** a gap *after a move, in the direction of that move*. It comes at the end of moves and leads to reversals.
  - Action: **fade it at the nearest S/R level**, if the profit margin is significant.

### Gap targets and gap levels
- Gap-down-into-demand long (p94-95): **target 1 = the origin of the gap; target 2 = the nearest pivot high.**
- An **unfilled gap from days earlier is a quality demand level**. The S&P later gapped down into that gap's origin and rallied most of the day (p160).

## 6. Time of day

- The largest order imbalance is at or near the open, so that is where the high-probability trades are (p113, p140).
- **Best window for active equity traders: the open until about 2 hours into the session** (p142).
- Do the S/D analysis **in the pre-market**. XLT sessions start **30-60 minutes before the open** (p134, p143).
- **Beginners should not trade the open** (p140, p143).

## 7. Multi-timeframe and market alignment

- **One-timeframe traders don't make consistent profits** (p97). The best 5-minute buy near HTF supply, or the best 15-minute sell near HTF demand, "is not likely to work."
- HTF trends begin and end at HTF levels (p97).
- **Review the weekly and daily charts at least once a week** (p98, p100). Three reasons:
  1. To know where trends end and begin, so you are first in line.
  2. To know which side of the market has the odds for active trades.
  3. To find swing and position trades (e.g. the GBPUSD short from May 2008).
- **Stocks: time entries with the S&P / NASDAQ levels (p113, p134-136).**
  - Spend about 5 minutes on index zones, then trade a handful of stocks at most.
  - Pick stocks whose levels line up with the index level (GOOG and RIMM supply lined up with S&P supply).
  - A **rapid move away** from the stock's level means very high probability.
- **Top-down scan (p157-159): index → large sectors → a handful of high-volume stocks.** All of them should sit at demand (or supply). If more than 3-4 candidates qualify, **choose the ones with the largest profit margin** (farthest from the opposing level). Seiden dropped automated scanners entirely.

## 8. Profit margin and R:R

- **Profit margin** = the distance from the entry level to the opposing level at the time of entry. For longs it is resistance minus support (p124, p143).
- "Without the presence of a significant profit margin, there is no trading opportunity" (p143).
- Minimum: **3:1** (p103). Worked example (RIMM): risk **$0.50** to make **$1.50** to the first target = 3:1 (p136).
- **Volatility** = the distance between demand and supply levels. Wide distances = high volatility, and volatility shrinks when the levels tighten (p111).

## 9. Entry, stop, target and management

- **Short at the proximal (lower) line of supply, buy stop just above the distal (upper) line** (p112). A long mirrors this.
- **Stop placement:** just above where all the sellers are, i.e. just beyond the zone's far line (p146).
- **Use stop-MARKET orders for protection, never stop-LIMIT orders.** A stop limit may not fill: if you bought at $41.00 with a stop limit at $40.50 and no buyers are there, the loss keeps growing (p145).
- **Trail stop from new supply and demand (p147).** Go to a smaller timeframe. Each strong decline candle creates new supply at its origin, so move the buy stop to just above that new supply as the short works. A long mirrors this.
- **OCO / "set it and forget it" (p129-130):** place the entry limit at demand, the protective stop and the profit limit all at once, then don't touch it. This removes emotion and screen time.
- **Pre-plan everything.** Mark demand below and supply above in the pre-market. Buy at demand and take profit (or reverse short) at the supply above (p127).
- **Position sizing (p148):** account **$100,000**, max risk **1% = $1,000**, stop **$0.40**, so **2,500 shares**. Use a position-sizing grid to stay mechanical.
- **Four outcomes:** big win, small win, small loss, big loss. **Eliminate the big loss** (p148-149).
- **Student stats (p149-150):** 7 wins / 17 losses = **29.17% win rate**, avg win $2,528.29, avg loss $265.43, **P/L ratio 9.53**, **max risk 2%** ($827.35), net **+46.79%** ($28,182 → $41,367.73) in under 3 months. A low win rate is fine if losses stay small and winners run to their pre-planned targets.

## 10. Trend

- Trend = the direction of **average prices**. Rising = odds with buyers; falling = odds with sellers. Trading with the trend always carries better odds (p123).
- But "proper trend analysis is about objective **anticipation of the next trend**" (p124). Trends end at HTF levels.

## 11. Curve / location

- Demand should be "a pivot low, well placed on the larger supply / demand curve" (p103).
- The three market states (p90-91): demand > supply (pivot low), supply > demand (pivot high), and equilibrium. Most candles sit at equilibrium, but **don't trade there**. Competition pushes price back toward equilibrium.

## 12. Principles, market mechanics and psychology

- **Three principles (p89-92):**
  1. Price movement in a free market is a function of an ongoing supply and demand relationship.
  2. Any and all influences on price are reflected in price.
  3. Price moves when one of the two competing forces becomes **zero** at a price.
  - Worked numbers: **300 buyers vs 200 sellers at $20.50**. Price looks stable until the 200th seller sells, then 100 buyers remain and price rises.
  - The earthquake analogy: two plates press until a threshold is reached.
- **The novice's two mistakes (p119):** (1) buying after a period of buying, or selling after a period of selling; (2) buying into supply, or selling into demand. Always ask, "Who is on the other side of my trade?"
- **CME pit story (p118-119):** take the other side of the visibly desperate, consistently losing trader. "Turns in the market happen when the novice trader has entered the market."
- **The task (p123):** (1) objective *anticipatory* analysis, entering before others; (2) low-risk entries.
- **Tools:** candles, trend, S/D.
- **The analysis:** trend → where S/D is → is there a profit margin?
- **News (p150-153):** bad news that pushes price into a pre-drawn demand level is a **buy**. The ADP report of 4/1/09 (-742k jobs vs -663k expected) sent the NASDAQ into a DBR demand level, and it then rallied. Filter the news through the chart: sell on bad news only if price is at supply.
- **Ignore the news entirely (p98, p101):** "If we as traders never heard any of it, would we have traded any different? Nope."
- **Macro rule (p111):** whenever a country announces a **bailout**, it is a "hard and set rule" in the XLT to find supply and short the stock market. This is opinion-heavy context, not an S/D rule.
- **Traps (p131-134):**
  - S&P short pre-set at **914** (supply 914-917) after the 905 breakout rally.
  - The **"red line" trap**: two old pivot lows sit just above a real demand zone. Retail buys at the red line, then sells when it breaks, selling straight into the real demand zone. Price then reverses fast. Enter close to the stop.
- **Market makers (p82):** institutions bid price up to known supply to invite the public in, then dump into it. The chart is "the one place a market maker or institution can't hide."
- **Psychology:** we are conditioned from birth to trade wrong (buying on good news, after an uptrend, with indicators pointing up). Good buys come at the end of downtrends, with bad news and indicators pointing down (p125-126).
- **Failure stats (p126):** about 80% fail because their education is wrong. Of the 20% who are properly educated, about 80% fail on discipline.
- **Trading as gambling with stacked odds (p102):** you see the cards before betting, and you choose the size.
- **Losses are emotionally neutral to him (p150).**
