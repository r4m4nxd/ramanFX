## 1. A Smart Trading Group (p.241-245)
- **Opening trades:** entering near the open is smart when the open goes straight into a demand or supply level, because the imbalance is largest then.
- **Exits at the higher-timeframe level of the sector or index:** a student exited stock trades when the matching ETF was $0.10 from a **60-minute** zone and made 3:1 and 5:1. Sam says "stick to your plan". No one catches the whole move, and reaching for more invites losses.
- **Expectancy:** "If you never did any better than 3:1 and only had winning trades 40% of the time", you would be very profitable. Per 5 trades that is 2 x 3R - 3 x 1R = +3R.
- **Slippage:** buying at demand means the broker on the other side "had to sell at demand". On spot FX and thin non-major crosses (GBPCHF example, about 10 pips of slippage on a market order after the limit order was not filled), expect slippage.
- **Set and forget:** use entry orders so you don't watch the screen and emotion stays out.
- **Volume at turns:** the most significant turns happen on **low** volume. The bigger the imbalance, the fewer trades and the less time spent at that price. He looks for low-volume turns. (A student had heard the opposite.)
- **Hesitation on entry:** accept that there is no certainty and look for "better odds". Have a rule-based plan and simply execute it.
- **Scaling size:** go from 10 to 100 shares, not to 1000. "Scale up too fast" is a top reason traders fail. Trading 10 shares while learning was the right move.

## 2. Market Traps (p.246-248)
- **Bull trap logic:** an event (news, or a popular pattern such as a breakout above the prior day's high) draws in the crowd to buy **while price is at objective supply with a big profit margin below**. After the last buyer, price collapses and trapped buyers' stops add to the fall. It is the event and the level together that make the trap.
- Example: S&P weekly rallies into the **origin of a gap down from the prior year**. Gaps show a strong imbalance. On the small timeframe, the breakout above the **prior day's high** into that supply is the trap. Sell to the breakout buyers, with T1 at the demand below.
- **Rules:**
  - A significant event invites buyers, price is at supply, and the profit margin below is adequate: sell short.
  - A significant event invites sellers, price is at demand, and the margin above is adequate: buy.
- Mental side ("car dealership"): you want to trade against first-time buyers, not professionals.

## 3. Conventional TA is flawed: the double top (p.248-252)
- Student AA short: daily supply = a pivot high followed by a **gap down** from it. He shorted on the return at 12.08, with **T1 10.85 = 3:1 and T2 10.03 = 5:1**.
- The textbook double top only becomes "official" near the low or neckline. Selling the neckline break means **selling after a decline and into demand**. The S/D trader sells the second top at supply instead, before the pattern completes.
- Customize conventional patterns by "wrapping real demand and supply rules around" them, so you enter before the pattern traders.

## 4. Mixed Feelings: the hard right edge game (p.252-256)
- The open is a **Doji after a gap up into obvious supply to the left**, so selling is the high-odds trade. Graduates mostly sell. New prospects copy whatever the first person does (herd behavior) and mostly buy. Price then fills the gap down.
- Graduates did **more than 400% better** than the new people.
- Sam is not worried about creating competition, because fear and greed keep most people buying where he sells.

## 5. Three trading styles (p.256-260)
- **Day trading:** needs fast data, direct access and several monitors. Consistently profitable day traders take **1 to 3 quality trades a day, usually near the open**, not 10 to 100. Emotions run high, you compete with market makers, and it takes the most time.
- **Swing trading (Sam's favorite):** hold from 1 day to a couple of weeks or longer. It is the least crowded niche, too long for day traders and too short for institutions. About **an hour of analysis 2 to 3 times a week**, done when the market is closed, then set and forget. Downside: setups come in bunches, for example when the S&P pulls back to demand most stocks set up at the same time.
- **Position trading:** weeks to months. It is "buy and hold AT THE RIGHT TIME". Trap: using news and opinions leads to buying high.
- The strategy is the same on every market and timeframe. Only the time horizon changes.

## 6. Questions on trading styles (p.260-263)
- Newton (motion until met by an equal or greater force) and Adam Smith (supply above demand means price declines) say the same thing: "the basics never die".
- **Swing timeframes:** a higher timeframe of **daily or weekly** to place price on the curve and pick the side, plus **60-minute "but no smaller"** to refine the entry and shrink the stop. "Focus on where the majority of the trading activity is" in the HTF zone. This is the turning point.
- Swing trading uses the same analysis as day trading: find levels, assess trend, plan, execute. It is just slower and done offline.

## 7. Out-thinking your competition (p.264-267)
- An edge has two parts: **mental** (reality-based thinking, self-control, focus) and **strategic** (a rule-based plan).
- Nov-Dec 2009 S&P: the supply was the **origin of a strong decline from Oct 2008**, carried forward with two lines. Each news-driven **breakout to a new high inside that supply** was sold. The student made more than $10K on ES. His process started from a clean chart with no indicators.
- **Three factors for the short:** (1) price returns to the origin of a strong decline, so buyers are buying after a rally and into supply; (2) breakouts to new highs inside supply, where "I would be bearish and a seller on BREAKOUTS TO NEW HIGHS within this supply level"; (3) **contrarian check of advisory services**: no competitor had spotted the level, and most were bullish, which added odds. If other services point out the same level, that means more competition for fills, which is bad.

## 8. Uses of S/D outside trading (p.267-271)
- Crude daily: short at supply to demand (more than 12 points), then long at demand. Fill the tank or hedge fuel at demand. An airline buying futures at the **$68 demand** level "flies at $68 while competitors fly at $73".
- 10-Year Note daily: short at supply that is high on the curve, after a **novice gap up** that followed a strong rally. Price and yield move inversely, so supply on the note is the time to lock a fixed-rate mortgage or refinance.

## 9. Don't Be a Blind Donor (p.271-274)
- Learn the mechanics (platform, contract specs) from books. Learn profitability from Econ 101 supply and demand, carried onto the chart as "retail vs wholesale" prices.
- Reader recap of known criteria: a level is not in the middle of a move, and more touches mean less reliable.
- **Departure ("how price leaves a level")** as an extra criterion: the more out of balance, the stronger the move away, while a gradual move away means a smaller imbalance. Search for **periods of momentum, then find their origin**.
- **Trend vs moving average:** a reader uses a 50 SMA on the daily to pick the side on 4H/60m. Sam: an MA is acceptable only as a **secondary** tool. The primary test is where price sits relative to HTF S/D, because trends begin and end at levels. He takes only levels that meet his plan's minimum R:R, and "When I find it, trend is a distant consideration."

## 10. A Key Rule, Often Overlooked: the qualifying rule (p.274-278)
- DBR = demand, RBD = supply. Most traders never **quantify with numbers** what counts as a level.
- **A significant profit margin matters for two reasons:** it measures risk and reward, and **a larger margin means higher probability** (price is far from equilibrium).
- **Rule:** "A demand level only becomes a demand level if the initial rally from the demand level is at least three times the demand level (1:3 Risk/Reward)." The zone height is the distance from entry at the proximal line to the stop just beyond the distal line. Example: a 2-point zone needs an initial rally of at least 6 points, otherwise ignore the level. Supply is the mirror: the initial decline must be at least 3 x the zone height.
- Your own minimum could be 1:4 or more. Meeting the rule makes **T1 = 1:3** likely. T1 does not have to be the top of the initial rally.
- Entry: buy at the **top (proximal) demand line**, with the stop **just below the lower (distal) line**.
- Examples: Crude daily demand (long, reached T1 and T2), and 10Y Note daily supply (the fast, large initial decline "absorbed" the demand below, so T1 was easy).
- Speed and length of the initial move are the key factors.

## 11. Planes, Trains and Automobiles: indicators as filters (p.278-284)
- 10Y Note daily short: supply well placed on the curve, the odds-enhancer exercise said high probability, and price **gapped into the level** (novice buyers). Price and volume alone were enough.
- **Bollinger:** a pierce of the upper band *and* arrival at supply with a large margin means higher odds. The Options XLT (Eric Ochotnicki) calls this an **"All Star" setup**. Never trade a band pierce on its own.
- **CCI:** overbought at **+100 or more**, oversold at -100 or less. CCI was overbought at the entry, but there were earlier overbought readings without a turn. CCI as the primary tool would have entered early and been stopped out, and would have exited too early. It is a decent **secondary** tool.
- **Slow Stochastics:** the cross in the extreme zone lined up with both entry and target, but it wasn't needed.
- Rule: filter every indicator signal through real S/D levels.

## 12. Trade What is Real: illusions, and the 3 laws (p.284-295)
- Beliefs lead to behavior patterns, which lead to actions. Fix the beliefs.
- **MA illusion:** 20 and 200 MAs on the S&P daily. At "B" (the first return to demand "A") the 20 MA slopes **down**, yet this is the best buy. Waiting for a cross back above the 200 MA means higher risk and less profit, and you end up paying the trader who bought at B. "When a moving average lines up with true demand or supply, the moving average will appear to work."
- **Buying the first revisit (B) guarantees three things:** the smallest stop (correct position sizing), the largest profit potential (the distance to the supply above shrinks as price rises), and the highest probability.
- Indicators are derivatives of price and volume. Price is enough.
- **News illusion:** the London bombings. Price fell into objective demand "A" and turned up. "No matter how bad the news is, when the last seller sells at a price level where there are more willing buyers, prices rise." Strong news creates turns *opposite* to what the majority expects, because one side exhausts itself into a level.
- **Fundamental illusion:** CTXS got a brokerage upgrade and gapped up into supply on the **first rally into it**, which is the worst place to buy. "The bigger the gap, the more herd mentality". "When perceived risk is lowest, actual risk is often highest" (and the reverse).
- The **two novice mistakes**: (1) buying after a period of rising prices, and (2) buying where there are objectively more sellers than buyers.
- "Act like a goose": execute mechanically and don't question it.
- **Three Laws of Price Movement:** (1) price is a function of the ongoing S/D relationship; (2) every influence on price is reflected in price; (3) the origin of a price move is where one of the two forces (buyers or sellers) **becomes zero** at a specific price.

## 13. Tools not found in books (p.295-299)
- Sam rejects higher-high / higher-low trend reading as lagging. He uses "real time trend analysis" but does not explain it here.
- **Trend strength from pivot spacing:** in an uptrend, if the **distance between successive pivot lows is decreasing**, price is nearing supply, the trend is weakening, buy risk is rising and the margin is shrinking, so "the uptrend is likely almost over". The downtrend mirrors this with pivot highs. A strong trend doesn't pull back often. This works on any market and timeframe.
- **Volume:** the most significant turns come on **low** volume (big imbalance, little time, few trades). He wants to see **below-average volume at the demand or supply entry point**.

## 14. Your Questions Answered (p.299-302)
- Use **2 to 3 timeframes** suited to your style. The HTF gives the curve and the side. The LTF gives the low-risk level.
- **Swing setup in XLT Futures:** **weekly + monthly** for the curve, then **daily + hourly** for levels with big profit margins.
- **One-stock trader (RIMM, opening-range idea):** (1) analyze the **index first** (NASDAQ/QQQQ), then match the stock's turning points to the index turns, which filters out weak levels; (2) a 5/15-minute-only trader needs a larger timeframe (60-minute or daily), because a perfect 5-minute buy at daily supply will likely fail; (3) qualify levels with the **"odds enhancers" checklist**.
- **Trend:** stop the long hunt for the perfect trend method. "Every trend begins and ends at price levels where supply and demand are out-of-balance". Be in before the HH/HL crowd buys.

## 15. System trading: switch + trigger (p.302-311)
- Most systems with 80% backtested win rates lose with real money because they are curve-fitted.
- Your system must find the **consistently losing trader** on the other side.
- **The two parts of a system:**
  - **Switch:** says "ok to buy" or "ok to sell", never both at once. Indicator version: the **50-period MA** slope. S/D version: **price reaching the level**.
  - **Trigger:** the actual entry. Indicator version: a slow-stochastic cross in the oversold or overbought zone, or a CCI reading. S/D version: a reversal candle at the level, price touching the level, or another trigger.
- **Summary matrix:**

  | Trend (50 MA slope) | Oscillator | Action |
  |---|---|---|
  | Up | Overbought | Ignore |
  | Down | Oversold | Ignore |
  | Up | Oversold | **Buy** |
  | Down | Overbought | **Sell short** |

- The logic: buy from the novice who sells *after a decline in an uptrend*, and short to the novice who buys *after a rally in a downtrend*. The same holds for QQQQ, NQ (CCI) and USDJPY (CCI).
- Adding indicators or re-optimizing after live losses is the path to disaster.

## 16. Got Questions? (p.311-315)
- **Sideways regime:** define "trend" by a **numeric MA slope threshold**. A slope between the up and down thresholds counts as sideways, and then take **both** oscillator buys and sells. Return to trend mode when the slope resumes.
- **Gap mid-move:** after a gap up, find the **nearest DBR to the origin of the gap**. It is often at the origin, but sometimes **just below** it, and that is the real entry. Supply mirrors this. (See also his "Gaps, Pro versus Novice" article.)
- **Supply above still matters:** before investing for a big move up, (1) find demand below with a significant upside margin, *and* (2) wait for some or all of the overhead supply to be **absorbed (traded through)**. Arriving at demand does not mean price will get past a big supply above.
- Hand charting (graph paper, colored pencils) makes levels obvious. Old-school tape readers buy where demand exceeds supply.

## 17. Patience and Probabilities, GOOG (p.315-319)
- Markets spend most of their time near equilibrium (no edge) and the least time at extreme imbalance (the edge), so patience is required.
- **GOOG swing short, March 12 2010, five odds enhancers:**
  1. **S&P nearing supply**, so most stocks will decline.
  2. **Novice buying in GOOG:** a gap up after a strong rally, into supply.
  3. **Gap down out of the GOOG supply**, a strong departure. "The stronger price moves away from a price level, the more out-of-balance supply and demand is at the origin".
  4. **VIX at demand**, meaning complacency: everyone has bought, so there are no buyers left.
  5. **Large profit margin** (demand far below). A larger margin means higher probability, like "the rubber band is stretched".
- Not every setup has every enhancer. Waiting for them takes discipline.
- He entered at the level, **before** the red confirmation candle. The next day price gapped down to T1. Confirmation traders who sell the next day help those already short.
- The text says "gap up in price into demand" on p.318, but the context makes clear this is supply (a typo in the source).

## 18. Patience and Probabilities, Part 2: INTC (p.319-320)
- When **day trading stocks, time the entry to key S&P and/or NASDAQ turning points**. INTC supply was sold when the S&P (cash and futures) also reached supply, with demand lower giving an "acceptable (not great)" margin.
- "Selling INTC short when the S&P or NASDAQ is not at a supply level is a very low probability trading opportunity."
- Supply = the origin of a price decline (the yellow zones on 3 INTC timeframes plus the S&P charts).
