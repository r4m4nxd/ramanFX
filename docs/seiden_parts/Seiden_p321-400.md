## 1. Levels on top of levels (pp. 321-324, 348, 367-368)

- **Rule:** a supply level with another supply level **just above it** (or demand with demand **just below it**) is usually stronger than a level standing alone. He calls it an **odds enhancer** (p.348, 367). Analogy: a bank vault with two steel doors. Order-desk view: a large stack of sell orders at 1070 and an even larger stack at 1071.
- It must still come with a **big profit margin** ("two supply levels ... with a big profit margin, that is better than having one").
- **How to enter (p.322-323):**
  - Option A, **scale in**: put part of the position at the lower (proximal) level and the rest at the upper level, with **one stop above the upper level**.
  - Option B: put the **whole position at the upper level**.
  - **Never** enter the whole position at the lower level with the stop just above the lower level. That puts your stop "right where you should be entering short".
- Example: SPY short at **$119.00**. Two stacked supply levels; the crowd ("Foolsville") was buying at the same price.

## 2. Market timing on the larger timeframes (pp. 324-328)

- **Profit margin on the big picture** = the distance between the **fresh** demand level and the **fresh** supply level. In March 2009 that distance was "huge", so the setup was the best R:R (XLT alert on 4 March 2009, monthly demand).
- **X-ray rule:** a large monthly level should be inspected on smaller TFs, like a doctor taking several x-rays of one elbow. Inside the monthly supply, the weekly showed **two levels, a proximal one and a distal one just above** (levels on top of levels again).
- If price breaks through the HTF supply anyway, do not fight it: "buy pullbacks to new demand levels".
- **News as a contrary input:** very bad news with price at or near demand means a turn higher. Very good news with price at or near supply means a turn lower.
- Buy a basket of stocks that are also at **larger-TF demand** (the member's trade). Index and stock levels should agree.
- Keep the analysis "real" and "simple".

## 3. All Star entry (pp. 328-331 "Lite"; full rules pp. 388-391)

- The setup combines **S/D levels with Bollinger Bands** (period and SD not given; standard settings are implied).
- **Short rules (p.390):** price reaches a key **supply** level, **pierces the upper Bollinger Band**, the market is **in the context of a downtrend**, and there is a **significant profit margin below**. Then sell short at the supply level with a **buy stop just above the supply level**.
- **Targets:** **T1 = the Bollinger midline (the mean); T2 = the opposing demand level.**
- Longs: the mirror image (demand + lower-band pierce + significant margin above).
- The "Lite" version (p.330) leaves out the trend condition. Sam says the full All Star has **two more rules that are not published** (XLT only). The 2010 version adds "in the context of a downtrend", which may be one of them.
- The logic is two independent extremes: a S/D imbalance plus a statistical extreme (mean reversion). Whoever buys outside the upper band, after a rally, at supply is the novice.
- Examples: RIMM (stock XLT), and INTC, which hit supply on the day of a *good* earnings report and reached T1 quickly.

## 4. Target placement: just before the opposing level (p.329)

- **Rule:** set the profit target **just before** the opposing level, not at it. When covering a short you want to buy just above demand, because at demand you compete with other buyers and may not get filled.

## 5. The Check List / Market Traps questions (pp. 332-337, 366)

Sam lists four questions, and the Q&A on p.366 adds a fifth ("how price returns to the level", answered in an earlier webinar):

1. **How did price leave the level?** Three grades, from weakest to strongest: **gradual move < strong move with big red/green candles < gap away (strongest)**.
2. **How much time did price spend at the level?** **Fewer candles is better.** A big imbalance means few transactions, so **few candles and low volume**. That is the opposite of textbook TA (heavy volume, many candles).
3. **Levels on top of levels?** (section 1)
4. **How far did price move before returning to the level?** Further means a larger profit margin and higher probability, because the level is far out on the S/D curve ("the rubber band is stretched"). The supply rally that was absorbed shows "where the sellers are, and where they are not".
   - **Target rule (p.337):** measure **from the supply level to the lowest low before price rallied back**. That distance is **one of the profit targets** (mirror it for demand).
- Freshness is the fifth filter (section 9).
- Entry is everything: entering at the turn gives the biggest size for the least risk and the biggest reward to target (p.335).

## 6. Weak departure can be offset (p.348)

- One short had a **weak** move away from supply, and he took it anyway because:
  1. the rally was huge and news-driven, so price was high on the curve;
  2. there was a **big profit margin below**, which was the main reason;
  3. it had **levels on top of levels**.
- The rule: several odds enhancers can make up for one weak one. The best levels have them all, but "we are not looking for perfection, we are looking for better odds" (p.366).

## 7. Origin vs body of the move; a gap is not the level (p.347)

- Big green candles and the gap area are the **movement**. The tradable level is the **origin** of the strong move.
- Even at an origin, **do not buy if there is supply level after supply level overhead**, because there is no profit margin. A missing margin cancels an otherwise valid origin.

## 8. Bandwagon candle (p.347-348)

- A **big red candle after a decline** (or a big green one after a rally) is the crowd ("bandwagon") selling into demand / buying into supply. It shows **late** participation, not a new level.

## 9. Freshness (pp. 353, 366, 368-369)

- **Definition:** each visit fills some of the resting orders, so willing buyers or sellers shrink with every touch.
- Trade the **first time** price returns, "or sometimes second". "After that, the level is not fresh anymore and the odds ... are decreasing rapidly."
- First-touch entries also need **a significant profit margin**.
- BP: Sam skipped the higher grey demand because it was **not fresh**. He took the lower fresh demand at **$26-28** (entry about **$27.00**, longer-term hold; member exited at **$38.47**, +$11).
- ES Q&A (p.366): **1083-1085** was rejected because there was "plenty of recent price action to the left" (not fresh), it sat higher on the curve, and its departure was weaker. **1073-1075** was preferred (with **1063-1065** below) because it was lower on the curve, had a bigger margin and a stronger rally. Result: a **13-point** rally off 1073-1075.
- Robot point: buy both levels with stops and targets and you get one loss, one win and a net profit. Picking the best levels is an edge, but you do not need to be right every time.

## 10. Old supply becomes new demand (S/R flip) (p.334)

- After demand absorbs all the supply and price breaks through, the old supply becomes demand, **but only if all three hold**:
  1. a **significant profit margin** from the new demand;
  2. the **breakout was strong**;
  3. the new level is **well placed on the larger-TF S/D curve**.
- If any one is missing, the new level is weak.

## 11. Trend (pp. 333, 395-397)

- Conventional trend (an MA pointing up) makes you buy after prices are already expensive. **Identify where the trend begins with S/D levels.**
- A larger-TF MA is fine **"as a general guide for trend ... but make sure it's not your primary indicator"** (p.333).
- In *Veil of Illusion* he is harder on it. The **20-period MA slope** was down at the best buy points ("B"), so the MA filter would have kept you out. Waiting for price to cross back above the **200-day MA** means higher risk, less profit and paying the people who bought at B. "When a moving average lines up with true demand or supply, the moving average will appear to work."
- Indicators and oscillators are only derivatives of price and volume. **Price is all you need.**

## 12. After-hours and index context for stocks (p.338)

- Include **after-hours / Globex levels**. Most line up with day-session levels anyway.
- **Rule for stocks:** check the overnight S&P futures chart. A level on a small-TF stock chart may be invalid because the S&P traded well beyond it overnight.
- Every level must meet minimum standards (the Check List) before it counts.

## 13. Cross-market confluence (pp. 373, 386-387)

- XLNX short at daily supply. **Entry $27.75, stop ~$28.50, target ~$25.50** (risk 0.75, reward 2.25, so **3:1**). Odds were higher because **QQQQ was also at supply**.
- Euro futures long at fresh demand at **1.3085**. Odds were higher because the **Euro was at daily demand while the US Dollar Index was at supply** (inverse correlation). He used the level for a couple of shorter-term trades worth about **$4,400**.

## 14. Government / central-bank intervention (pp. 340-344)

- From his CME floor years (BOJ yen intervention): price first moves the way the intervention intends, then returns to pre-intervention levels and goes **much further the other way**. "Intervention in free markets never works."
- **XLT rule:** every time a **bailout** is announced, look for an S&P **supply** level and get ready to short.
- 21 June 2010: the Yuan "intervention" headline gapped the S&P up about **1.5%** (Dow +1.30%, Nasdaq +1%). He shorted the first small supply that formed on a small TF, for three reasons: novice news buying, price at supply, and **the gap left no demand below, so there was a large profit margin**. The gain was small but the R:R was ideal.

## 15. News rules (pp. 349-365, 398-400)

- The "buy criteria" taught in school (good company, strong earnings, healthy balance sheet, quality management, uptrend, MAs sloping up) are only all true **when the price is already high**. "Paying $50,000 for the $30,000 car."
- INTC 15 April 2010: EPS of 43c vs 38c consensus, revenue +44%. Price **gapped up into the origin of a strong decline** (the first return to that supply), and swing targets below were reached by 6 May.
- **Two rules (p.361):**
  1. negative news that brings price to **fresh demand** is usually a buy, not a sell;
  2. positive news that brings price to **fresh supply** is usually a sell, not a buy.
- "Bad news + a collapse in price = a low risk, high reward buying opportunity". The last seller exhausts at the imbalance level (BP **$27.00**).
- Novice double mistake: **buying after a rally AND into supply** (or selling after a decline into demand).
- Test question: "If I buy the stock here, will someone buy it from me higher?"

## 16. Level 2 / order-book data (pp. 369-372)

- Level II, Total View and the Matrix **do not show real intentions**. AAPL 2-min example: Sam's rules said sell at supply while the book showed **1,600 bid vs 200 offered**, and price collapsed seconds later.
- ES always shows hundreds to thousands on the bid and offer, but usually only **1-2 contracts trade**.
- Real order-flow visibility would be illegal to trade on. On the CME desk he could not trade his own account and was watched on camera and recorded phones.
- "Who can you trust? ... Trust price."

## 17. Price and time (pp. 373-377, 391-394)

- **Time at level** gets its own article. Markets turn hardest where the imbalance is largest, and that means **few candles and low volume**, not many candles and high volume.
- Example: an S&P supply of **four little candles** that held on the retrace.
- **Two-level example (p.392):** supply level 2 had many candles and a sharp drop, so textbook TA loves it. On the revisit, price based sideways and broke through it. XLT rules **ignore** levels like this ("not a supply level at all"). Supply level 1 had only a few candles, was barely visible, and held.
- SPY idea (p.377): a strong initial drop, little time in the supply, and **an empty area with no demand below** (easy travel). So expect a pause or a reversal.

## 18. The Bull Trap (pp. 381-385)

- **Setup:** a **fresh supply level sits just above an obvious high**, here the **Globex (overnight) high** of the ES. When the regular session trades above the Globex high, traders read it as a breakout and buy into the fresh supply.
- **Trade (Pro Picks, 17 Nov 2010):** short ES **1182**, stop **1184.25** (**2.25 points** of risk). 1182 was the high of the day, and the targets were met.
- A Bear Trap is the mirror image (breakdown below an obvious low into fresh demand).
- Underlying idea: hunter vs hunted. Know how the crowd is trained to think (buy new highs) and sell to them at supply.

## 19. Ten-Year Note futures (TY) (pp. 377-381)

- A favourite market because it is **very liquid**, so levels are "very solid and easy to identify" ("you can almost draw a box around that level").
- 15-min supply after sideways trading; the first rally back was shorted for **$750** (cut short, he had a flight to catch).
- Bond prices up means rates down. Investors use the larger-TF levels.

## 20. Psychology and belief system (pp. 356-365, 394-395)

- **Your belief system is your strategy.** Invitations to buy and sell (news, indicators, earnings, upgrades) must be filtered through the market's real S/D before you act.
- Two kinds of invitation: the **market's own** (S/D, which is reality) and **everything else** (illusion).
- Rule-breaking is usually not a discipline problem. It is a **conflict between the rules and your beliefs**, so fix the beliefs instead of punishing yourself.
- Beliefs lead to behaviour patterns, which lead to actions. Use the "shopper's belief system" (coupons, car dealers, half-price sale) in the markets: buy at wholesale (demand), sell at retail (supply).
- A strategy that works is **neither market-specific nor time-period-specific**, and "market conditions" changing is itself an illusion (p.394).
- Questioning conventional wisdom ("ask the right questions") is the source of an edge.

## 21. Beginners (pp. 334-335)

- Pick a style (day / swing / position). Then use a **simulator** until three things are proven:
  1. you are comfortable with the platform;
  2. you can execute the plan;
  3. the rules are profitable.
- Then start with **QQQQ** (cheap, very liquid) and a **small position size** until the data says you are ready.

## 22. Universality (p.339)

- S/D ("motion into mass") applies to every market and asset class, Asia included. He uses "supply/demand" instead of "resistance/support" because S/R means different things to different people (pivots, Fibonacci, Elliott, MAs).
